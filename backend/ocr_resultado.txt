ABSTRACT

Gamification is widely used in educational systems to enhance engagement and learning experiences. However, its effects on
learners’ emotions remain underexplored. This study investigates the impact of gamification on students’ basic emotions and
emotional transitions during interaction with a learning management system (LMS). A controlled between-subjects experiment
was conducted with 62 university students using either a gamified or non-gamified LMS. Emotional responses were captured in
real time through automated facial-expression recognition and analyzed by duration and frequency. Anger was the most frequent
emotion and fear the least frequent in both conditions. Statistical analyses found no significant differences between groups in
emotion duration or frequency. However, emotional transition analyses revealed distinct patterns: anger-to-happiness was
the most common transition in the gamified condition, whereas disgust-to-anger predominated in the non-gamified condition.
These findings suggest that the specific gamification design used in this study did not affect basic emotional expressions but
may have influenced emotional dynamics during learning. The study contributes objective evidence on emotions in gamified
learning environments and highlights the importance of examining emotional transitions beyond static emotional states.

Introduction

Gamification, i.e., “the design of systems, services, and activities to provide motivational benefits similar to those games
usually create”!-2, has garnered significant interest from researchers and practitioners as a strategy for promoting positive
behavioral change**. In learning systems, gamification has been widely adopted to enhance user engagement, motivation, and
learning outcomes**. By incorporating gameful dynamics such as acknowledgment, storytelling, and narratives, designers
and educators aim to create more interactive and enjoyable learning experiences, potentially fostering positive emotions and
improving learning outcomes’,

Despite the widespread adoption of gamification in learning systems and the promising results reported in various studies”,
the link between engaging in learning with a gamified system and the learners’ emotional states has seen limited attention in
academia!®. Past research appears to have focused more on learners’ cognitive and behavioral outcomes, and less on emotional
outcomes!!.!2, which limits the understanding of how gamified environments affect students’ emotions!?. Understanding how
gamification influences students’ basic emotions, ¢.g., happiness, sadness, anger, surprise, fear, and disgust'*, is crucial!5. 16, as
emotions play a significant role in learning and motivation to learn!” '8.

Addressing this gap, we investigated the effects of gamification on students’ basic emotions (i.e., happiness, sadness, anger,
surprise, fear, and disgust) and emotional transitions, thus answering the following research questions: RQ1: Does gamification
affect the total duration of students’ emotions during LMS interaction? RQ2: Does gamification affect the frequency of
students’ emotions during LMS interaction? RQ3: What are the sequential relationships between students’ emotions during
LMS interaction? These RQs compare emotional duration and frequency across gamified and non-gamified LMS versions and
identify emotional sequences by examining which emotions precede others and how these patterns differ between conditions.
These insights can support the design of emotionally supportive gamified learning environments.

To answer these research questions, this study employed a controlled between-subjects experimental design involving 62
university students who interacted with either a gamified or a non-gamified version of the same LMS. Participants completed
identical learning tasks while their emotional responses were continuously monitored using automated facial-expression
recognition software. Six basic emotions (happiness, sadness, anger, surprise, fear, and disgust) were analyzed in terms of
duration, frequency, and transition patterns. Statistical analyses were then conducted to compare emotional experiences across
conditions and to identify the most frequent emotional sequences during the learning process.

Our findings show that anger was the most commonly observed emotion across both system versions, while fear was
the least expressed in both. However, the analysis revealed no significant differences in emotional expressions between the

implementation rather than as evidence against the emotional effectiveness of gamification as a broader design approach.

RQ3: What are the sequential relationships between students’ emotions during LMS interaction?

Given the exploratory nature of the emotional transition analysis and the relatively limited sample size available for sequential
analyses, transitions were examined descriptively rather than through inferential statistical modeling. Consequently, the reported
transition frequencies should be interpreted as preliminary indications of potential emotional dynamics rather than statistically
validated differences between conditions. Future studies with larger samples should apply transition probability models,
sequential analyses, or Markov-based approaches to determine whether the observed emotional pathways differ significantly
across learning environments.

An initial analysis of the transitions between emotions experienced by participants in the experimental group reveals distinct,
repeating transition patterns. The most frequent transition occurred from angry to happy (19 times), indicating that episodes
classified as anger were frequently followed by expressions classified as happiness during system interaction. Although this
pattern may reflect emotional shifts occurring while participants interacted with the system, the present data do not allow
conclusions regarding the causes underlying these transitions.

Discussion

In an effort to fill a gap in the field of gamification in education, which is to understand how gamification affects learners’
emotions, in this study, we analyzed the effects of gamification on students’ emotions. Our primary finding was the absence of
statistically significant differences in the measured emotional expressions between participants interacting with the gamified
and non-gamified versions of the LMS. Importantly, this finding should be interpreted as the absence of detectable differences
under the conditions of this study rather than definitive evidence that gamification does not affect learners’ emotions. The
statistical interpretation of the findings should consider the sensitivity of the study design. While the sample size was adequate
for detecting large between-group differences, smaller emotional effects commonly observed in educational technology
interventions may not have been identified. Accordingly, the present findings provide stronger evidence against the existence of
large emotional effects than against the existence of more subtle emotional influences of gamification.

An important observation is that anger emerged as the most frequently detected emotional state across both experimental
conditions. While this finding may indicate moments of frustration associated with task completion, alternative explanations
should also be considered. Previous research has suggested that automated facial-expression recognition systems may
occasionally classify facial configurations associated with concentration, cognitive effort, problem-solving, or task-focused
attention as anger-like expressions?. Therefore, the high prevalence of anger observed in this study should not necessarily be
interpreted as evidence that participants were predominantly experiencing negative affect. Instead, it may reflect a combination
of genuine frustration, increased cognitive effort, and limitations inherent to facial-expression-based emotion detection. Future
studies combining facial analysis with self-report measures or physiological sensors could help clarify the nature of these
emotional states.

One possible explanation for the lack of significant emotional differences is the design of the gamification elements
themselves. It is crucial to assess whether these features were sufficiently engaging or if participants perceived them as
superficial. The effectiveness of gamification largely depends on the quality and relevance of the game mechanics used‘. If the
elements did not resonate with users or align with their intrinsic motivations, they may not have elicited the intended emotional
responses.

An important consideration when interpreting these findings is that the present study did not include a direct manipulation
check assessing participants’ perceptions of gamefulness, engagement with the gamification elements, or perceived differences
between the two system versions. Consequently, although the gamified version included ten gamification elements, we cannot
determine the extent to which participants actively perceived or engaged with those features. Therefore, the observed null
findings may reflect either a genuinely limited emotional impact of the implemented gamification design or insufficient
differentiation between the subjective experiences produced by the two versions of the system.

Another factor to consider is the duration of the experiment. The time participants spent interacting with the gamified
system may not have been sufficient to produce noticeable emotional changes. Emotions are transient and influenced by various
factors?!, which may include gamified elements. A longer-term study could reveal different emotional response patterns as
participants become more accustomed to the gamified environment.

The nature of the educational tasks used in the study may also have played a role. If the tasks were not sufficiently
challenging or engaging, they might not have elicited strong emotional reactions, regardless of the gamification?. Ensuring

that tasks align with participants’ interests and skill levels is critical in determining the emotional impact of gamification.

Additionally, individual differences among participants, such as prior experiences with gamification, personal preferences, and
emotional baseline, could have influenced the results”>-*>. Some participants may have been more receptive to gamification
than others, potentially diluting the overall emotional impact when averaged across the entire sample.

The results reveal recurring emotional transition patterns, with certain emotional states appearing sequentially more
frequently than others (see Figures 2a and 2b). A notable observation is the bidirectional transition between anger and happiness,
with 19 instances of participants shifting from anger to happiness and vice versa. This finding indicates that anger and

co-occurred within participants’ emotional trajectories during system interaction. However, no conclusions can be drawn
regarding engagement levels based solely on these transition frequencies.

Threats to validity and limitations

This study identified several threats to validity and limitations, each with potential implications for the interpretation of our
findings. Although measures were taken to mitigate these challenges, some limitations remain inherent to the study design.
Initially, while participants were randomly assigned to the experimental and control groups, the manual method of turning a
cup for group assignment could introduce bias or errors. To mitigate this, randomization was conducted immediately before the
experiment in the presence of two researchers, minimizing the potential for systematic bias.

We relied on Noldus Observer XT and FaceReader for detecting and analyzing emotional responses. Although these tools
are widely regarded for their accuracy and reliability, any inherent inaccuracies could affect the results. To address this, all
equipment was calibrated and tested according to the software manuals before use. At the same time, potential confounders,
such as prior experience with gamified systems, individual motivation levels, or familiarity with the educational content, were
not controlled. Standardized tasks and uniform instructions aimed to minimize these effects. However, residual influence
remains a possibility.

Another limitation concerns treatment fidelity. Although participants in the experimental condition interacted with a version
of the LMS containing gamification elements, the present study did not directly assess the extent to which participants noticed,
used, or actively engaged with individual gamification features. Consequently, we cannot determine whether all participants
similarly experienced the gamified environment. Future studies should incorporate system log data and behavioral interaction
metrics to investigate participants’ engagement with specific gamification elements and their relationship with emotional
responses.

An additional consideration concerns the interpretation of facial-expression recognition data as indicators of emotional
states. Although FaceReader is a widely used and validated tool for automated affect detection, the broader literature contains
ongoing debate regarding the extent to which emotional experiences can be reliably inferred from facial expressions alone.
While basic-emotion theories suggest that facial movements can provide meaningful indicators of underlying emotions,
alternative perspectives argue that emotions are context-dependent constructions that may not map consistently onto specific
facial configurations. Consequently, the emotional measurements reported in this study should be interpreted as estimates
derived from observable facial-expression patterns rather than as direct measurements of participants’ subjective emotional
experiences.

Another limitation is that the present study did not directly assess learning outcomes, focusing instead on emotional
experiences during system interaction. Consequently, it is not possible within this specific study to determine whether the
observed emotional patterns were associated with differences in learning performance. Future research should continue to
investigate the relationship between emotional dynamics and educational outcomes using integrated analytical approaches.

The sample size of 62 participants provided sufficient statistical sensitivity to detect large effects but offered more limited
power for identifying small or moderate effects. Consequently, while the analyses do not support the existence of large
emotional differences between conditions, smaller effects may have remained undetected. Therefore, the absence of statistically

occurrence of several emotional states, transition analyses were conducted descriptively. Therefore, although the observed
patterns provide useful insights into potential emotional dynamics during system interaction, they should be interpreted
cautiously and should not be viewed as evidence of statistically significant differences in emotional trajectories between the

Theoretical implications

This study advances our understanding of the outcomes of engaging with gamified learning tools and supports the existing
research that has focused on cognitive and behavioral outcomes!!- 12.26 by including the perspective of emotional outcomes. We
provided empirical evidence on the impacts of one gamified learning tool on the basic emotions'* of happiness, sadness, anger,
surprise, fear, and disgust in a controlled laboratory setting, which contributes to the growing body of research investigating
emotions in gamified environments through objective measurement approaches. In our empirical study, there were no statistically
significant differences in experienced emotions between a group using a standard version of the tool and a group using a
gamified version of the tool. Despite this, we saw multiple different emotions manifest rather consistently across the participant
pool. This finding indicates that the gamification design implemented in the present study may not play as significant a role as
could be assumed based on previous research (e.g.,2”-°). However, it remains unclear whether we would see larger differences
with heavier gamification approaches or games that build on elements such as fictional narratives, world building, social
challenges, and immersive gameplay? 230.

The present study deliberately focused on emotional processes rather than learning outcomes. This decision was motivated
by the specific objective of examining how students’ emotional states evolve during interaction with a gamified learning
environment and by the existing emphasis of the gamification literature on cognitive and performance-related outcomes”! .
Importantly, learning outcomes were investigated separately within the broader research project from which this study emerged.
Previous work using the same experimental context identified positive effects of gamification on students’ academic performance,
suggesting that improvements in learning outcomes may occur even in the absence of substantial differences in basic emotional
expressions. Taken together, these findings highlight the importance of examining emotional and learning-related outcomes as
complementary, but not necessarily equivalent, dimensions of the gamified learning experience.

The use of automated emotion detection in this study aligns with the broader goals of affective computing within smart
educational contexts. By leveraging objective, real-time data from FaceReader and eye-tracking, this research moves beyond the
limitations of subjective self-reports to provide a high-resolution map of the learner’s emotional state. These findings contribute
to the theoretical understanding of how ’smart’ systems can perceive human affect, suggesting that while gamification elements
are identifiable by the system, they do not fundamentally alter the primary emotional intensity elicited by the instructional
content itself. This underscores a need for theoretical models in smart education that prioritize the interplay between automated
physiological sensing and core task design.

this body of literature through empirical evidence on transitions between emotions while engaged with a gamified learning
system. We show that there are both bidirectional and unidirectional relationships between basic emotions, and this opens the
need for more research into the shifting emotional states and their relationship with learning. These findings relate to work on
the importance of linking learning attributes to game mechanics, a crucial link that has been addressed in depth in previous
research. In light of this body of literature, it is also important to examine the quality of the gamification implementation
concerning the observed student emotions.

Another possible explanation for the lack of significant emotional differences between gamified and non-gamified conditions
is through the Self-Determination Theory, where motivation and engagement are driven by the core psychological needs of
autonomy, competence, and relatedness*>. The gamification elements may have been perceived as rigid or externally imposed,
which would make students feel the game is controlling instead of motivating, reducing internal motivation, and increasing
neutral responses. This may be mitigated by adaptive gamification elements that allow students to select rewards and set
personal goals. They may have also felt little sense of progress due to the lack of more immediate and clear feedback (high
levels of anger to surprised and angry to happy in the experimental group, in Figure 2a), and a possible lack of achievement
(frequently transitioning from happy to angry in Figure 2a). Adding more challenge-based gamification elements, scaffolded
difficulty in our questionnaires, and more meaningful feedback may help increase competence-driven engagement. Finally,
happiness and surprise may increase in the experimental group with an increase in relatedness by adding more socially related
gamification elements like team-based challenges and cooperative quests”.

The lack of significant emotional differentiation between the gamified and non-gamified groups suggests that ‘meta-game’
interactions, typical of many current smart platforms, may lack the depth required to influence basic achievement emotions.
Theoretically, this indicates that for gamification to be effective in an interactive smart environment, it must be ‘intrinsic’,
embedded directly into the learning mechanics rather than added as a reward layer. This finding encourages a shift in the design
of interactive technology away from extrinsic motivators, like leaderboards and points, toward smart instructional designs that
gamify the cognitive challenge itself, thereby fostering a more authentic and emotionally resonant interactive experience.

Practical implications

Our work provides two practical implications for gamified learning system design. First, since in our empirical context
gamification appeared to have a non-significant impact on basic emotions, designers could focus more on other aspects, such as
cognitive load management and meaningfulness of the learning tasks, to foster positive emotions in participants. Of course,
gamification is a broad umbrella term for various solutions, and our empirical findings offer only perspectives on one context,
so we encourage practitioners to apply these findings through trial, experimentation, and testing. Second, the discovered
transitions between emotional states could be more knowingly manipulated, and designers could even look forward to mapping
emotional state pathways for learners where key learning content is tied to moments of positive emotions. Designers could
also examine system interaction logs alongside emotional data to identify which gameful features are associated with specific
emotional trajectories. Such information may help designers refine gamification implementations and better understand how
learners engage with individual elements throughout the learning process.

A key practical contribution for designers of smart learning environments is the identification of specific emotional transition
patterns, such as the frequent shift from anger to happiness observed in this study. In a truly “smart” system, these detected
transitions can serve as algorithmic triggers for adaptive interventions. For instance, if an automated system detects that a
student is lingering in a state of frustration (anger) without transitioning to a positive state (happiness), it could proactively
adjust the difficulty level or provide scaffolded feedback. Utilizing these objective transition matrices allows developers to
move toward "emotionally-aware” interfaces that respond dynamically to the user's real-time psychological needs rather than
relying on static gamification wrappers.

Our work also provides some insights into UX research practices at companies beyond the realm of gamification. Today,
multiple companies engage in UX research efforts to better understand how various product design elements are likely to be
received, or are actively received, by the target audience”. We demonstrate that we can observe differences in emotions
between participants using a gameful system and a non-gamified version of it. Such data could be useful for companies
carrying out A/B testing to identify which version is better received by target audiences*?, or help UX researchers identify
user pain points*!. While our study was conducted in a controlled laboratory environment, similar studies could potentially
be done remotely as well, with specific instructions provided to participants regarding how they should set up a camera to
film themselves. Such studies can offer game and learning environment UX researchers new tools for understanding their
participants’ experiences.

Research agenda

For future research, we encourage replicating this work with other learning systems to better understand what context-dependent
phenomena may have appeared in our data. For example, it could be that the learning system we used provokes an unusually
high amount of anger in participants, and that the basic emotions experienced with a competing system look different. Such
studies would add more robustness to our findings and contribute important additional empirical evidence for building more
generalizable theoretical frameworks for understanding the impacts of gamification on learners’ emotions in an educational
setting.

We also encourage future studies to map specific emotions to specific instances in learning scenarios and to examine
participants’ interactions with individual gamification elements through system logs and behavioral analytics. Such analyses
would enable researchers to investigate treatment fidelity and identify which gamification features are actually perceived
and engaged with by learners, thereby improving our understanding of how specific gameful affordances relate to emotional
experiences.

Another avenue for future research relates to the way emotions are detected. According to2, the affective neuroscience
approach, emotions are fundamental and shared across mammalian species. There are circuits in our brain specifically
responsible for certain emotions, and these emotions not only direct our behavior, but can be recognized reliably from facial
expressions*2. While this was the approach we took in this paper,” challenges the fundamental assumption of emotions having
dedicated brain circuits and being recognizable from facial expressions in her popular theory of constructed emotion.? argues
that emotions are constructed in the brain as the outcome of the complex interplay between different brain regions, and that part
of emotions is learned, or the way emotions manifest can be impacted by learned information”.

The theory of constructed emotion challenges the approach that we can reliably detect emotions simply from people’s faces.
What this means for our research is that future research could investigate alternative approaches for emotion detection (such as
self-reports and bodily sensors) to boost the reliability of the findings. Furthermore, we encourage scholars to examine the
fundamental assumptions they make in the study of emotions in the context of gamification and to see whether emotions are
indeed ubiquitous or whether we see evidence of the manifestation of individual differences.

Finally, emotional responses should be further examined outside the controlled lab environment, such as classrooms
or home-based e-learning contexts, as emotional engagement may differ in naturalistic educational settings, due to social
interactions and external stressors?0*4. While we acknowledge the challenges associated with this type of approach, such
studies have the potential to yield unprecedented insights for the community by enabling an understanding of students’ emotions
in an ecological context.

Methods

Study Design

In this study, we investigated the effects of gamification on students’ basic emotions. To achieve this, we conducted a between-
subjects controlled experiment. By conducting this study, we aim to move beyond responses centered on students’ subjective
experiences and gain a clearer understanding of the effects of gamification on their emotions. Data collection was conducted
in a usability laboratory between November and December 2023. The controlled lab environment enabled monitoring of
physiological and emotional responses during system interaction and allowed for immediate post-task interviews. In this study,
we did not aim to understand exactly which specific aspect of gamification causes a particular emotion.

Participants

Participants were recruited via the Tampere University DMLab pool, utilizing ORSEE3 software for coordination?, an internal
system at Tampere University used exclusively to invite students to participate in studies conducted by university researchers.
Due to the interdisciplinary nature of the study, no prior exclusion criteria were used. After data collection, we filtered outliers
with data imputation, applying the mean value for each row with a Z-score larger than 2.5. The final sample, excluding outliers,
comprised 62 students from 19 countries; 33 identified themselves as female, 26 as male, two as non-binary, and one preferred
not to respond. The average age was 25 years, with a standard deviation of 5.00 and a variance of 26.00. Participants had never
used the platform before. Each participant received a movie ticket as compensation (i.e., each ticket cost 8 euros). None of the
participants had prior contact with the gamified LMS used in the system or with the researchers involved in the study. The
Institutional Review Board approved the study, and participants provided informed consent for participation and recording.

Materials

We employed the gamified educational system Eagle-edu (https: //eagle-edu.com.br/) to conduct our study (i.e.,
present the educational tasks for the participants). We chose this system because of its adaptability, which empowers educators
to create diverse educational activities. The system incorporates 21 distinct gamification elements, which align with the
Taxonomy of Gamification Elements for Educational Environments (TGEEE) proposed by*º. These elements can be selectively

For the experimental group, the gamification design comprised 10 gamification elements, aligned with the taxonomy
proposed by Toda et al;

* Acknowledgment: This element provides feedback recognizing specific player actions. In our system, this was
represented by student badges.

Chance: Linked to randomness and probability, this element affects the likelihood of certain outcomes.

Competition: Arising when multiple players compete for a shared goal.

Economy: This element involves game transactions, including the monetization of values. Our system featured coins as
currency for in-game purchases.

* Imposed Choice: Players had to make decisions to progress in the game. Our system offered random options to increase
rewards.

* Level: Representing hierarchical progression, levels in our system were categorized as Bronze, Silver, Gold, Ruby, and
Diamond.

Objective: Goals, either quantifiable or spatial, guided players in the game. In our system, a quest tree structure
represented the objectives.

* Points: Points measure user performance, with experience points (XP) serving as the unit of measurement.
* Progression: This element tracked player advancement through a progress bar integrated into the activity tree.

* Stats: Displaying a player’s results and progress, this element provides visible information on all user progress data.

The objective of the present study was not to isolate the effects of individual gamification elements but rather to evaluate the
emotional responses associated with a realistic gamification design composed of multiple interacting elements. Consequently,
individual engagement with specific elements was not analyzed separately. Examples of the system’s graphical user interface
are provided in the supplementary materials.

Procedure

The study was conducted in three key steps: i) random assignment, ii) system interaction, and iii) emotions assessment. In
the first step, participants were randomly assigned to either the experimental or control group. The randomization process
was carried out with the participant (manually) immediately before the start of the experiment, after the student entered the
laboratory, where participants were asked to turn a cup over. Inside that cup, there was a paper indicating whether the participant
would be in the experimental or control group. The participant was not informed which group they would be in. In the
second step, system interaction, participants engaged with the system for at least 30 minutes, completing educational tasks and
exploring its features. In the third step, emotion assessment, data were collected during system usage of the software Noldus
Observer XT (i.e., through equipment attached to the participants’ bodies). Figure 3 summarize the study’s procedure.

Measurement

In our study, we utilized Noldus Observer XT (https: //www.noldus.com/observer—xt—human) and FaceReader

selected for their robust capabilities in behavioral coding and facial expression analysis, critical for accurately capturing and

Data analysis
To ensure the appropriate selection of statistical tests, we began by assessing the distribution of the collected data. Given the
sample size of N = 62, we applied the Shapiro-Wilk test”. Developed by Shapiro and Wilk“, this test is particularly suited for
small to moderate sample sizes'?. It computes a W statistic and p-value that compares the observed data to what would be
expected under a normal distribution. A high p-value, i.e., p > 0.05, suggests that the data may be normally distributed”.
Then, according to the Shapiro-Wilk test results (i.e., p < .001 for all emotions except neutral emotion p = .198), we
conducted a Student’s t-test’ for normally distributed emotions’ data (i.e., where data do not reject the null hypothesis of
normality) and a non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test for the others (i.e., where data reject the null hypothesis of normality)
to compare the means between the experimental and control groups, both using the two-sided approaches>!. Because no
statistically significant differences were observed across the tested emotions, correction for multiple comparisons would not
affect the interpretation of the results.
